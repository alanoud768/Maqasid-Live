Introduction: help or stereotype?

When travellers disclose a disability, does ChatGPT help them or steer them toward stereotyped choices? Disclosing a disability may improve accessibility, but may also limit activities. None of the 26 reviewed studies tested this. Our study addresses this gap.

Methodology

We used 144 synthetic places in Riyadh, 15 accessibility rules and 3 simulated travellers, and analysed 2,000 ChatGPT responses.

Figure 1. Dataset preparation and the accessibility filter

The top row shows how the places were made and checked. Below it, the places, their categories and the 15 rules go into the filter, which marks each place, for each traveller, as fully suitable, suitable with a gap, or excluded. ChatGPT never saw the verdicts.

One sentence, two different answers

The traveller asked for history and heritage places. The highlighted sentence, “I use a wheelchair”, was the only change. Without the sentence, 4 of 5 places were unusable in a wheelchair. With the sentence, 5 of 5 places were suitable for a wheelchair user.

Figure 2. Stating the disability cut unsuitable picks by up to 56 points

The bars show answers, out of 100, with an unsuitable place. Grey is not stated, and green is stated. For the wheelchair user, 89 fell to 33. For the blind traveller, 40 fell to 1, and for the Deaf traveller, 31 fell to 3.

Answer to research question 1: disclosure cut unsuitable picks for all three travellers; our filter removed the rest.

Implications: who benefits

Travellers with disabilities: more control over what they disclose to the model. Recommendation platforms: combine a sourced rule filter with disclosure. Tourism in Saudi Arabia: publish accurate, detailed facility information.

Prototype: the accessibility filter

Python if-then rules pick usable places; ChatGPT then chooses.

Conclusion

Check essential needs with clear rules first. Then let the AI choose.

Next steps: real places, more models, Arabic prompts.
