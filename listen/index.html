Introduction: help or stereotype?

When travellers disclose a disability, does ChatGPT help them or steer them toward stereotyped choices? Disclosing a disability may improve accessibility, but may also limit activities.

Who our study benefits: travellers with disabilities, recommendation platforms, and tourism in Saudi Arabia.

Research questions

Research question 1, disability disclosure: how does disclosing a visual, hearing, or mobility disability change ChatGPT's recommendations in accessibility, budget, interest relevance and diversity?

Research question 2, stereotyped steering: does disclosure steer travellers away from suitable places, compared with no disclosure?

Research question 3, accessibility filter accuracy: how accurately does a rule-based filter classify places using published accessibility guidelines?

Research question 4, filter-first approach: how does filtering without disclosure compare with disclosure on the same four measures?

Method

Figure 1 shows our method. We generated 144 places in 12 categories, gave each place 23 facilities, removed unlikely cases, and ran 11 data checks. Our accessibility filter then checked every place against 15 rules, marked Essential or Important, and gave a verdict for each of the three travellers: a wheelchair user, a blind traveller and a Deaf traveller. The verdicts made the filtered lists and scored every answer.

The model, GPT-6.1 Sol, saw only the place descriptions and listed facilities, never the verdicts. Each request was sent as a new conversation. The places were shuffled in each round, and the same order was used for all groups.

For the blind traveller, 40 fell to 1. For the Deaf traveller, 31 fell to 3. All three differences were statistically significant.

Research question 3.

RQ3Our filter was 100% accurate in every test

It passed 35 of 35 logic tests and 11 of 11 data checks, and 0 of 600 answers were unsuitable after the filter. Figure 4 shows its decisions for the 144 places. For each traveller, 72 places were fully suitable. The rest were suitable with a gap, or excluded: 48 were excluded for the wheelchair user, 20 for the blind traveller, and 12 for the Deaf traveller.

Conclusions · main message

Subtle bias, real consequences

Travellers may be offered unsuitable places, and miss experiences they could have enjoyed.

A need is not an assumption

A traveller's need is specific and checkable. A model's assumption is invisible and untested. For example, a traveller says “I need step-free entry”, but the model assumes “They won't enjoy an outdoor market”. A traveller says “I need captions”, and the model says “Skip the concert hall”. A traveller says “I need an audio guide”, and the model says “Museums are safer”.

Our filter checks the needs with stated rules, so the model is free to suggest what each traveller might enjoy.

Our contributions

An audit of disability disclosure in the model's travel advice; a transparent, sourced accessibility filter; and evidence that filter plus disclosure works best.

Limitations and next steps

Synthetic places and simulated travellers; one model, one city, English prompts only. Next: real places, more models, Arabic prompts.
