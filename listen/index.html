Bias in ChatGPT Recommendations of Synthetic Riyadh Tourist Places for Travellers with Visual, Hearing, and Mobility Disabilities, and the Effect of a Rule-Based Accessibility Filter

What we asked

When a traveller tells ChatGPT about a disability, does it help her, or steer her toward stereotyped choices? None of the 26 studies we reviewed had tested this.

We built 144 fictional places in Riyadh, each listing 23 accessibility facilities, and wrote 15 accessibility rules. Then we asked ChatGPT for 5 places, 2,000 times, for a wheelchair user, a blind traveller and a Deaf traveller, with and without stating the disability.

Figure 1: our method

The top row shows how the places were made and checked. Below it, the places, their categories and the 15 rules go into our accessibility filter, which marks every place as fully suitable, suitable with a gap, or excluded, for each traveller. ChatGPT saw only the place descriptions, never these verdicts.

Table 1: the groups

Group B is the control, with no disability stated. Group A states the disability. Group C uses only our filter, and group D uses the filter and states the disability. Two follow-up tests either showed only fully suitable places, in E1, or hid the facility information, in E2.

The example: one sentence, two answers

The same request was sent twice. Without the sentence “I use a wheelchair”, 4 of ChatGPT's 5 places were unusable in a wheelchair. With it, all 5 were suitable.

Figure 2: research question 1

The bars show answers, out of 100, with a place the traveller could not use. Grey is without stating the disability, and green is with it. For the wheelchair user, 89 fell to 33. For the blind traveller, 40 fell to 1, and for the Deaf traveller, 31 fell to 3.

The prototype

Python if-then rules pick the usable places, and ChatGPT then chooses among them.

Conclusion

Check essential needs with clear rules first. Then let the AI choose.

Next, we plan to test real places, more models, and Arabic prompts.
